Predicting Content Underperformance: A Client-Grouped Evaluation of a Refresh Opportunity Ranker

Worked example paper — written as an intern would submit it. Companion artifact to the Week 7–8 capstone.

Abstract

Problem. A central editorial team at FlyRank maintains 118,092 content pages across a pseudonymized client portfolio. The decision this system supports is the order of a monthly refresh review: which pages should editors inspect first when capacity is fixed? Setting. The source is a 201,699-row BigQuery warehouse release (Jan–Mar 2026), reduced to 118,092 eligible pages after a visibility floor (impressions ≥ 100). Evaluation uses client-grouped holdout (GroupShuffleSplit). Approach. We compare a transparent four-rule baseline (old + never optimized + thin + no keyword) against LightGBM, XGBoost, and CatBoost on identical folds, with 50+ engineered features around freshness, depth, and bucket-relative performance. Evidence. CatBoost achieves ROC AUC 0.850 and Precision@50 0.820 on the grouped holdout, versus the rule baseline at 0.400 — a 2.05× lift in top-50 hit rate. The base rate is 0.385. A final queue of 118,092 pages carries confidence tiers (high: 16,806; medium: 42,240; low: 59,046) and human-readable reason codes. Scope. This is a predictive ranker, not a causal claim; no A/B test evidence is included. All identifiers are hashed; no URLs or titles are exposed.

1  Introduction and claims

This work originated in the FlyRank ML Internship content-refresh lane. The practical decision is not "will this page underperform?" in the abstract — it is "which fifty pages should a person look at first this month?" The current editorial process relies on simple age rules; our ranker replaces intuition with a measured ordering.

We make three testable claims:

  1. C1. On a client-grouped holdout, the best gradient-boosting model achieves Precision@50 = 0.820, more than double the transparent rule baseline at 0.400 (§4, Table 1).
  2. C2. The evaluation boundary is load-bearing: a random split would inflate scores by letting the model memorize client-specific patterns; grouping by client_hash_id is essential (§3.5).
  3. C3. The top model features are bucket-relative comparisons (search_volume_vs_bucket_mean, content_age_days_vs_bucket_median), showing the model learns peer context rather than absolute thresholds (§4.3).

2  Data and task

2.1  Unit, source, period

One observation is one content item for one client. Source: the pseudonymized FlyRank internship warehouse release on HuggingFace Datasets, 201,699 daily fact rows spanning 2026-01-01 to 2026-03-31. The evaluation frame is 118,092 eligible items after filtering. Identifiers are pseudonymous hashes used for grouping and joining only — never as features.

2.2  Label

is_below_peer_median = (ctr < median_ctr_per_pos_bucket) — an observed outcome relative to peers in the same position bucket. The label's ingredients are quarantined columns that never enter the feature frame. Development base rate: 0.385.

2.3  Eligibility and exclusions

Eligible rows: ≥100 impressions across the window — floors that keep CTR estimates meaningful. Excluded outright: ctr, total_clicks, ga4_sessions, avg_position, pos_bucket (used only for target creation), and any label-derived fields. The dedup step removed 0 rows because the upstream DuckDB query already aggregates to one row per page.

2.4  Leakage controls

Three boundaries, each tested in code: (i) feature windows end strictly before any label computation; (ii) clients never cross fold boundaries (GroupShuffleSplit); (iii) all features are knowable at the decision instant. A deliberately leaky column list is maintained as a test fixture; the production feature frame refuses it.

3  Methodology

Feature engineering (50+ features, 10 ordered stages). We import the production pipeline from 01_prepare_features.py: has-flags, content depth, market opportunity, freshness scores, ratio features, age non-linearities, visibility, position bucket (for relative features only — dropped before training), bucket-relative comparisons, and interaction terms. The exact count is reproducible from the script.

Baseline. A transparent rule-based score: old + never optimized + thin + no keyword. It is not used as a feature; it is blended at evaluation time (30% baseline + 70% model) to keep the final queue explainable.

Validation. GroupShuffleSplit (80/20) by client_hash_id. The honest question is: "does it work on a client it never saw?" Seed 42 for every stochastic component.

Models. Logistic regression, random forest, LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost. CatBoost is selected for final retraining due to stable threshold behavior; XGBoost had the highest Precision@50.

5  Limitations

  • Causality, not prediction. The model predicts which pages currently have below-median CTR. It does not prove that refreshing a page will raise its CTR.
  • Position-bucket medians are noisy for small buckets. Buckets 51–100 and 100+ have fewer pages; their median CTR estimates have higher variance.
  • GA4 data is incomplete. Only a minority of pages have GA4 sessions > 0. Session features are excluded to avoid selection bias.
  • Single time window. Trained on Q1 2026 only. Seasonal trends or client mix shifts are not captured. A production system would retrain monthly.
  • Anonymized identifiers. We use hashed IDs only. We cannot verify content quality or keyword mapping without joining back to internal tables.
  • Threshold sensitivity. Precision@50 is reported at K=50. A mentor reviewing the top 25 will see a different hit rate. The high-confidence threshold is a business choice, not a statistical law.
  • Feature importances are model-specific. The top features shown are from CatBoost. LightGBM and XGBoost rank features differently.

Limitations, plainly

This is a predictive ranker, not a causal claim. I did not measure whether acting on the queue changes CTR — that needs an A/B test. One 3-month window; seasonal shifts are not captured. Past the top 50, precision naturally degrades.

What I would test next

  1. A rolling monthly retrain on new warehouse releases — the highest-value missing experiment.
  2. A controlled refresh experiment on high-confidence rows to measure real CTR impact.
  3. SHAP values for model-agnostic feature explanation across all three boosters.

Why this matters

Many SEO teams face the same meeting: traffic is drifting somewhere in a big content portfolio, and someone has to decide which pages get attention. In this worked example, capacity is a fixed monthly review budget. So the useful output is not a prediction score. It is a defensible ordering of the review list, with a reason on every row a human can inspect.

What we measured

One row = one page on one client site. "Underperforming" = CTR below the median of its position bucket (1–3, 4–5, 6–10, 11–20, 21–50, 51–100, 100+). In the development window, 38.5% of eligible pages underperformed — that number matters, because any method should be read against the random-selection expectation from that base.

Data reality. This is Search-Console-visible data with an eligibility floor (≥100 impressions). Small pages are noisy and are filtered out. Absence from this queue is not evidence a page is healthy. All identifiers are hashed; no URLs, titles, or raw queries are exposed.

The method, in plain English

First a baseline rule, built by checking signals one at a time: old pages, pages never optimized, thin pages, and pages without keyword data. Each earns a point. Transparent and auditable: on the full frame, the rule scores Precision@50 = 0.400.

Then a small model (gradient-boosted trees) over 50+ features a page already has at decision time: freshness, depth, market opportunity, and bucket-relative comparisons. The model does one job: it orders the queue. The rule keeps its job too: it explains each row. Model = order, rule = reason.

Glossary in one breath — base rate: how often underperformance happens anyway · precision@50: of the first 50 picks, how many were right · client-grouped test: we never test on a site the model trained on · leakage: letting the answer sneak into the features (we test for it in code).

Did it work?

On the client-grouped holdout, the model's Precision@50 is 0.820 and the rule's is 0.400 (random-selection expectation 0.385). The model more than doubles the rule's hit rate at the head of the queue. CatBoost was selected for retraining due to stable threshold behavior, though XGBoost had the highest single P@50 (0.860).

What to do with this

Finding. Pages that are old AND never optimized dominate the high-confidence queue.

What this means. In this dataset, age without optimization is the strongest underperformance signal. These pages merit review, not an automatic rewrite.

Action. Inspect the top 50 high-confidence rows. Verify word count, update history, and keyword alignment before editing.

Do not. Do not bulk-refresh every old page — some old pages perform at or above peer median.

Finding. The "thin content" flag appears in 59,000 rows — the most common weakness.

What this means. Thin content is widespread, but not all thin pages underperform. The model's bucket-relative comparison separates the real underperformers from the merely short.

Action. Prioritize thin pages that also have low CTR relative to their position bucket. Expand content where the gap is largest.

Finding. Pages with good impressions and position ≤ 20 but low CTR are strong refresh_and_review_ctr candidates.

What this means. The content body may be fine; the title or meta description may be the problem.

Action. Test title/meta changes before rewriting the full article. Measure CTR change over 2–4 weeks.

What we refused to automate: no auto-publishing, ever; every row carries a human-readable reason code; the model supplies the order, the rule supplies the explanation. High-confidence rows are the safest first-pass; medium and low confidence rows should wait for editorial capacity.

Limits — including the strongest counterargument

This is one portfolio, one label definition, and one 3-month window. The strongest counterargument to the whole approach: since CTR mean-reverts around position buckets, many "underperformers" recover untouched, so a queue that finds underperformers may partly find volatility. We agree — which is exactly why the recommended workflow is score → human review → hypothesis → controlled test, and why nothing here claims causality. Every number above is observed or measured; the decisions it supports are ordering decisions.
